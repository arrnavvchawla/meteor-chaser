Choosing where and when to watch a meteor shower: a model and a multi-objective method

Vinod Chawla (Arrnavv) · UCLA · November 2026 · version 1.1 · source

AppProblemFrontierValidationLimits

Abstract. An observer at a point oo on Earth wants to see as many meteors as possible on a given night, and wants to drive as little as possible to do it. We model the expected number of meteors seen from any site ss during any window [t, t+W][t,\,t+W] as a product of five factors: shower activity, radiant geometry, sky darkness, weather and observer sensitivity. Darkness depends on geography (artificial light from every town within 400 km) and topography (elevation). Weather is the forecast low, mid and high cloud. We then pose site choice as a bi-objective problem, maximise expected count and minimise drive time, over a finite candidate set built on real roads. We compute its Pareto frontier exactly. We prove that the app's single recommendation, a weighted-sum scalarisation, always lies on the frontier but can only reach the frontier's convex-hull vertices. That is why the whole frontier is shown. We report what has been verified (ephemeris to 0.07°, frontier properties by exhaustive test) and what has not yet been validated (the meteor-rate model against observations, the darkness model against meter readings).

Status labels used below: proved a mathematical statement with proof; verified checked numerically against an independent reference; standard taken from the meteor-science literature; heuristic a modelling choice with stated constants, not yet validated; open needs data.

1. The problem

Let o=(φo,λo)o=(\varphi_o,\lambda_o) be the observer's starting point, dd a night, WW the hours the observer will watch, and Dmax⁡D_{\max} the longest one-way drive they accept. A site ss is a point on a road reachable from oo. A plan is a pair (s,t)(s, t): a site and the start of a watch window [t,t+W][t, t+W] that night.

Two quantities matter to the observer:

  • N(s,t)N(s,t), the expected number of meteors they will see, which they want large;
  • τ(s)\tau(s), the one-way drive time from oo to ss, which they want small.

Everything else (cloud, darkness, elevation, moon, the shower's peak) is a cause of NN, not a separate goal. Section 10 shows how to add comfort and risk as goals of their own. The problem is

max⁡(s,t)∈P (N(s,t), −τ(s)),P={(s,t):s∈S, τ(s)≤Dmax⁡, t∈Td}, \max_{(s,t)\in\mathcal P}\ \bigl(N(s,t),\,-\tau(s)\bigr), \qquad \mathcal P=\{(s,t): s\in S,\ \tau(s)\le D_{\max},\ t\in\mathcal T_d\},
(1)

a bi-objective program over a finite set SS of candidate sites (section 8) and a finite set Td\mathcal T_d of window starts (section 7). For each site the window is chosen first, because τ\tau does not depend on tt:

t∗(s)=arg⁡max⁡t∈TdN(s,t),N∗(s)=N(s,t∗(s)). t^*(s)=\arg\max_{t\in\mathcal T_d} N(s,t), \qquad N^*(s)=N\bigl(s,t^*(s)\bigr).
(2)

This reduction is exact: if (s,t)(s,t) is Pareto-optimal for (1) then t=t∗(s)t=t^*(s), since otherwise (s,t∗(s))(s,t^*(s)) has the same τ\tau and a larger NN. The problem becomes a bi-objective choice of site, max⁡s(N∗(s),−τ(s))\max_s (N^*(s), -\tau(s)).

2. Time and sky geometry

All positions are computed from the Julian date JJ. The Sun follows Meeus' low-precision series [1]: with T=(J−2451545)/36525T=(J-2451545)/36525,

L0=280.46646∘+36000.76983∘T,M=357.52911∘+35999.05029∘T,λ⊙=L0+C(M), L_0=280.46646^\circ+36000.76983^\circ T,\quad M=357.52911^\circ+35999.05029^\circ T,\quad \lambda_\odot = L_0 + C(M),

where CC is the equation of centre. Meteor-shower times are catalogued by solar longitude in the J2000 frame, so we remove precession since J2000:

λ⊙,2000=λ⊙−1.3970∘ T. \lambda_{\odot,2000} = \lambda_\odot - 1.3970^\circ\,T.
(3)

The Moon uses the leading ten terms of the ELP series as given by Meeus, a topocentric parallax correction h↦h−0.95∘cos⁡hh\mapsto h-0.95^\circ\cos h, and illuminated fraction I=12(1−cos⁡E)I=\tfrac12(1-\cos E) from the Sun–Moon elongation EE. Radiants are catalogued in J2000 and precessed to the date with the first-order rates m=0.012811∘m=0.012811^\circ/yr and n=0.005567∘n=0.005567^\circ/yr:

α←α+(m+nsin⁡αtan⁡δ) y,δ←δ+ncos⁡α  y. \alpha \leftarrow \alpha + (m + n\sin\alpha\tan\delta)\,y,\qquad \delta\leftarrow\delta + n\cos\alpha\; y .
(4)

Altitude above the horizon at latitude φ\varphi, longitude λ\lambda, with Greenwich sidereal time θ(J)\theta(J) and hour angle H=θ+λ−αH=\theta+\lambda-\alpha:

sin⁡h=sin⁡φsin⁡δ+cos⁡φcos⁡δcos⁡H. \sin h = \sin\varphi\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos H.
(5)

verified Against astropy at five sites on four continents, both hemispheres, the largest altitude error is 0.01° for the Sun, 0.07° for the Moon and 0.00° for a radiant (table V1). An error of 0.1° changes sin⁡h\sin h by at most 0.0017, negligible next to the other uncertainties.

4. From ZHR to expected count

The IMO defines the zenithal hourly rate from an observed count nn over effective time TeffT_{\mathrm{eff}} (hours), corrected to a radiant at the zenith, a limiting magnitude of 6.5 and a clear sky [3]:

ZHR=nTeff⋅F  r 6.5−LM(sin⁡hR)γ, \mathrm{ZHR}=\frac{n}{T_{\mathrm{eff}}}\cdot\frac{F\; r^{\,6.5-\mathrm{LM}}}{(\sin h_R)^{\gamma}},
(7)

where F≥1F\ge 1 corrects for the fraction of the field blocked by cloud, hRh_R is the radiant altitude, and γ≈1\gamma\approx 1. Inverting (7) gives the rate a standard observer should see, which is what we want to predict. With γ=1\gamma=1 and F=1/TF=1/\mathcal T, where T\mathcal T is the sky transparency of section 6:

Shower rate. For shower jj, site ss, time tt, observer factor kk,
ρj(s,t)=ZHRj(λ⊙(t))⋅max⁡(0,sin⁡hR,j(s,t))⋅rj LM(s,t)−6.5⋅T(s,t)⋅kmeteors/h. \rho_j(s,t)=\mathrm{ZHR}_j\bigl(\lambda_\odot(t)\bigr)\cdot \max\bigl(0,\sin h_{R,j}(s,t)\bigr)\cdot r_j^{\,\mathrm{LM}(s,t)-6.5}\cdot \mathcal T(s,t)\cdot k \quad\text{meteors/h}.
(8)

Each factor has a plain meaning. ZHR is how busy the stream is. sin⁡hR\sin h_R is the geometric loss when the radiant is low: the observer's patch of atmosphere intercepts fewer meteors. rLM−6.5r^{\mathrm{LM}-6.5} is the loss from a brighter sky: for every magnitude lost, a fraction 1/r1/r of meteors remain visible. T\mathcal T is cloud. kk is the observer (section 12).

Background meteors. Sporadic meteors and minor showers add a rate that rises through the night as the observer turns toward Earth's direction of motion. heuristic We use

ρ0(s,t)=[6+4cos⁡2π(ϑ−6)24][1+0.25 σcos⁡(λ⊙−210∘)]⋅3.0 LM−6.5⋅T⋅k, \rho_0(s,t)=\Bigl[6+4\cos\tfrac{2\pi(\vartheta-6)}{24}\Bigr]\Bigl[1+0.25\,\sigma\cos(\lambda_\odot-210^\circ)\Bigr]\cdot 3.0^{\,\mathrm{LM}-6.5}\cdot\mathcal T\cdot k,
(9)

with ϑ\vartheta the local mean solar time in hours and σ=±1\sigma=\pm1 for the northern or southern hemisphere. This gives about 2/h in the evening and 10/h before dawn at LM 6.5, matching the AMS figures of about 2/h evening and about 9/h in the last hour before dawn [4]. The seasonal term is a rough northern-autumn maximum.

Total rate: ρ(s,t)=ρ0(s,t)+∑jρj(s,t)\rho(s,t)=\rho_0(s,t)+\sum_j \rho_j(s,t). Bright meteors (magnitude 2 or brighter, our working definition of "noticeable") are counted with fj=min⁡(1, rj 4.5−LM)f_j=\min(1,\,r_j^{\,4.5-\mathrm{LM}}). heuristic The offset 2.5 approximates the drop in a human observer's detection probability for meteors near the limit; it is not fitted.

5. Limiting magnitude: darkness, twilight, moon, altitude

5.1 Artificial sky glow (geography)

Walker's law [5] states that the zenith sky glow from a city of population PP at distance dd km, as a fraction of the natural sky brightness, is 0.01 P d−2.50.01\,P\,d^{-2.5}. We sum over every town ii with Pi≥15,000P_i\ge 15{,}000 within 400 km (33,961 towns, GeoNames), with an extra fitted factor cc:

β(s)=0.01 c∑i: di≤400Pi max⁡(di, ai, 1)−2.5,ai=Pi/(3000π) km, \beta(s)=0.01\,c\sum_{i:\,d_i\le 400} P_i\,\max(d_i,\,a_i,\,1)^{-2.5},\qquad a_i=\sqrt{P_i/(3000\pi)}\ \text{km},
(10)

where aia_i is the radius of a town of density 3,000 people/km², so the sum stays finite inside a town. The sky brightness in magnitudes per square arcsecond, with a natural sky of 21.9, is

SQM(s)=21.9−2.5log⁡10(1+β(s)). \mathrm{SQM}(s)=21.9-2.5\log_{10}\bigl(1+\beta(s)\bigr).
(11)

The single parameter c=0.3c=0.3 was fitted by least squares to five Southern California reference sites (table V2). open The reference values are approximate readings of published light-pollution maps, not meter measurements; see section 13.

5.2 Naked-eye limit

Sky brightness is converted to naked-eye limiting magnitude with the empirical relation in common use with Unihedron SQM meters [6]: standard

NELM=7.93−5log⁡10(10 4.316−SQM/5+1). \mathrm{NELM}=7.93-5\log_{10}\bigl(10^{\,4.316-\mathrm{SQM}/5}+1\bigr).
(12)

5.3 Altitude, twilight and moonlight (topography and time)

The working limit adds three corrections, with zz the site elevation in metres, h⊙h_\odot the Sun's altitude, and hM,Ih_{\mathbb M}, I the Moon's altitude and illuminated fraction: heuristic

LM= NELM+min⁡(0.4, 0.15 z1000)−min⁡(4, 0.35 (h⊙+18∘))+−I(0.3+1.2sin⁡hM)+, \begin{aligned}\mathrm{LM}=\ &\mathrm{NELM}+\min\Bigl(0.4,\ 0.15\,\tfrac{z}{1000}\Bigr)\\ &-\min\bigl(4,\ 0.35\,(h_\odot+18^\circ)\bigr)^{+}-I\bigl(0.3+1.2\sin h_{\mathbb M}\bigr)^{+},\end{aligned}
(13)

where (⋅)+(\cdot)^{+} means the term applies only when the Sun is above −18° or the Moon is above the horizon. The altitude term stands for the thinner, cleaner air over a mountain (less extinction, less haze), capped at 0.4 magnitudes. The twilight term removes about a third of a magnitude per degree of Sun altitude above astronomical twilight. The moon term removes up to 1.5 magnitudes for a full Moon overhead. All three are first-order approximations chosen to match observing experience; none is fitted. For h⊙>−8∘h_\odot>-8^\circ all rates are set to zero.

6. Cloud

The forecast (Open-Meteo, hourly) gives low, mid and high cloud fractions cL,cM,cH∈[0,1]c_L,c_M,c_H\in[0,1]. Assuming the three layers overlap at random, the fraction of sky clear of all three is (1−cL)(1−cM)(1−cH)(1-c_L)(1-c_M)(1-c_H). High cloud is mostly thin cirrus, which dims meteors rather than hiding them, so it counts half: heuristic

T=(1−cL)(1−cM)(1−0.5 cH). \mathcal T=(1-c_L)(1-c_M)(1-0.5\,c_H).
(14)

When only total cover cc is available (offline mode), T=1−c\mathcal T=1-c. Equation (14) treats cloud as a deterministic fraction of sky lost. A sky that is 30% cloud for the whole night and a sky that is clear 70% of the time give the same expectation, but very different variances; see section 10.

7. Expected count, window and uncertainty

Time is sampled every Δ=10\Delta=10 minutes from 17:00 local time for 15 hours: tm=t0+mΔt_m=t_0+m\Delta. The expected count for a window of n=W/Δn=W/\Delta steps starting at step ii is the left Riemann sum

N(s,i)=∑m=ii+n−1ρ(s,tm) Δ,t∗(s)=ti∗,  i∗=arg⁡max⁡iN(s,i). N(s,i)=\sum_{m=i}^{i+n-1}\rho(s,t_m)\,\Delta, \qquad t^*(s)=t_{i^*},\ \ i^*=\arg\max_i N(s,i).
(15)

Discretisation error. If ρ\rho is Lipschitz in time with constant LL (meteors/h²), the Riemann error is at most 12LWΔ\tfrac12 L W\Delta. For a shower rate climbing from 5 to 15/h over 3 hours, L≈3.3L\approx 3.3 and the bound is 0.3 meteors for a 3-hour window, small next to the Poisson spread below. proved

Uncertainty. Meteors arrive as a Poisson process, so given the model, the count seen X∼Poisson(N)X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(N), with standard deviation N\sqrt N and Pr⁡(X≥m)=1−∑x<me−NNx/x!\Pr(X\ge m)=1-\sum_{x<m}e^{-N}N^x/x!. Model error is larger: we quote ±40% on NN until the model is validated (section 13).

Cost. For ∣S∣|S| sites, 7 nights, 91 time steps and 15 shower terms, the model makes about ∣S∣×7×91×15≈1.4×106|S|\times 7\times 91\times 15\approx 1.4\times 10^{6} evaluations of (8) for ∣S∣=150|S|=150; the window search adds O(∣S∣⋅91⋅n)O(|S|\cdot 91\cdot n). This runs in under a second in a browser.

8. Geography: candidates, land, roads

Reach. The maximum straight-line radius searched is R=max⁡(15, Dmax⁡−860⋅801.32)R=\max\bigl(15,\ \tfrac{D_{\max}-8}{60}\cdot\tfrac{80}{1.32}\bigr) km: an 8-minute start-up, 80 km/h, and a road-to-line ratio of 1.32.

Candidates. Sites are placed on seven rings j=1,…,7j=1,\dots,7 at radius Rj=R (j/7)0.9R_j=R\,(j/7)^{0.9}, with nj=round(18⋅j7⋅1.6)+6n_j=\mathrm{round}(18\cdot\tfrac{j}{7}\cdot1.6)+6 equally spaced bearings offset by 11j∘11j^\circ, plus the origin: 158 points, denser near home where small moves matter. Each point is the destination along a great circle of Earth radius 6371 km. Points in water are removed with a 0.05° global land mask (GLOBE).

Roads. In live mode the OSRM table service [7] returns, for every candidate, the driving time from oo and the nearest point on the road network. Each candidate is moved to that point. It is dropped if the snap distance exceeds 6 km (open sea, roadless wilderness) or no route exists. Darkness (10) is recomputed at the new point. τ(s)\tau(s) is the OSRM time × 1.05 for night caution. Offline, τ\tau is estimated as 1.32 d/v(d)⋅60+81.32\,d/v(d)\cdot 60+8 minutes with v=50v=50, 72 or 85 km/h for d<40d<40, <120<120 or longer. Sites with τ>1.08 Dmax⁡\tau>1.08\,D_{\max} are excluded.

Topography. Elevation z(s)z(s) (Open-Meteo) enters the limiting magnitude (13) and the forecast temperature. Terrain blocking the horizon is not modelled; for a high radiant this matters little, for a low one it can matter a great deal.

9. The Pareto frontier

Write each site as the point x(s)=(τ(s), N∗(s))x(s)=\bigl(\tau(s),\,N^*(s)\bigr) in the plane, cost on the horizontal axis and reward on the vertical.

Definition 1 (dominance). Site aa dominates site bb, written a≻ba\succ b, if τ(a)≤τ(b)\tau(a)\le\tau(b) and N∗(a)≥N∗(b)N^*(a)\ge N^*(b), with at least one inequality strict.
Definition 2 (frontier). The Pareto frontier is F={s∈S: ∄ a∈S, a≻s}F=\{s\in S:\ \nexists\,a\in S,\ a\succ s\}.

The app computes FF by direct comparison of all pairs, O(∣S∣2)O(|S|^2), which is exact for a finite set.

Proposition 1 (the frontier exists and covers everything). For finite non-empty SS, F≠∅F\neq\varnothing, and every b∉Fb\notin F is dominated by some a∈Fa\in F.
Dominance is irreflexive and transitive, so it is a strict partial order on a finite set. A strict partial order on a finite non-empty set has maximal elements; those are exactly FF. If b∉Fb\notin F, some a1≻ba_1\succ b. If a1∉Fa_1\notin F, some a2≻a1a_2\succ a_1, and so on. By transitivity and irreflexivity no element repeats, so the chain ends after at most ∣S∣|S| steps at some a∈Fa\in F, and a≻ba\succ b by transitivity.
Proposition 2 (the frontier is a staircase). If no two sites share both coordinates, then sorted by τ\tau the frontier is strictly increasing in both τ\tau and N∗N^*.
Take a,b∈Fa,b\in F with τ(a)≤τ(b)\tau(a)\le\tau(b). If N∗(a)≥N∗(b)N^*(a)\ge N^*(b), then aa dominates bb unless both coordinates are equal, which is excluded; contradiction. So N∗(a)<N∗(b)N^*(a)<N^*(b). And τ(a)=τ(b)\tau(a)=\tau(b) with N∗(a)<N∗(b)N^*(a)<N^*(b) would make b≻ab\succ a; so τ(a)<τ(b)\tau(a)<\tau(b).

So walking along the frontier, every extra minute of driving buys strictly more meteors. Off the frontier, it doesn't: some other site is both closer and better.

9.1 One recommendation from the frontier

The app picks one plan by maximising meteors minus the value of round-trip driving, where v≥0v\ge0 is the observer's stated worth of one hour of driving, in meteors:

Uv(s)=N∗(s)−v⋅2 τ(s)60,sv∗=arg⁡max⁡s∈SUv(s). U_v(s)=N^*(s)-v\cdot\frac{2\,\tau(s)}{60},\qquad s_v^*=\arg\max_{s\in S}U_v(s).
(16)

This is the weighted-sum scalarisation of (1) [8,9]. vv is a marginal rate of substitution: the observer is indifferent between one more hour on the road and vv more meteors.

Proposition 3 (the pick is efficient). For every v>0v>0, every maximiser sv∗s_v^* of (16) lies on FF. For v=0v=0, at least one maximiser does.
Suppose v>0v>0 and s∗∉Fs^*\notin F. Then some a≻s∗a\succ s^*: τ(a)≤τ(s∗)\tau(a)\le\tau(s^*) and N∗(a)≥N∗(s∗)N^*(a)\ge N^*(s^*), one strictly. Because v>0v>0, UvU_v is strictly increasing in N∗N^* and strictly decreasing in τ\tau, so Uv(a)>Uv(s∗)U_v(a)>U_v(s^*), contradicting maximality. For v=0v=0, U0=N∗U_0=N^*; among the sites with the largest N∗N^*, the one with the least τ\tau is on FF.
Proposition 4 (which frontier points can be picked). As vv ranges over [0,∞)[0,\infty), the set of picks {sv∗}\{s_v^*\} is exactly the set of vertices of the upper-left convex hull of FF in the (τ,N∗)(\tau,N^*) plane. A frontier point strictly below a hull edge is never picked, for any vv.
Maximising N∗−v30τN^*-\tfrac{v}{30}\tau is maximising the linear functional ⟨(−v30,1),x⟩\langle(-\tfrac{v}{30},1),x\rangle over the finite point set {x(s)}\{x(s)\}. The maximum of a linear functional over a finite set is attained at a vertex of its convex hull, on the face whose outward normal is (−v30,1)(-\tfrac{v}{30},1). As vv runs from 0 to ∞\infty, the normal sweeps every direction with non-negative vertical and non-positive horizontal component. The hull vertices met are those of the upper-left chain, which by Proposition 3 lie in FF. A point strictly inside the hull is never the unique maximiser of a linear functional. The pick changes exactly when v/30v/30 equals the slope of a hull edge.

Consequence. A single slider can never recommend an "unsupported" frontier site: one that is a fine compromise but sits in a dent of the frontier. That is why the app draws the whole frontier and lets the user click any point on it. In the worked example (section 11), 7 sites are on the frontier and only 4 of them can ever be the pick. To reach any frontier point by a single parameter, use the ε-constraint form instead [8]: max⁡N∗(s)\max N^*(s) subject to τ(s)≤ε\tau(s)\le\varepsilon, which recovers every element of FF as ε\varepsilon varies over the frontier's τ\tau values.

Knee. With both axes rescaled to [0,1][0,1] over the frontier, τ~,N~\tilde\tau,\tilde N, the knee is arg⁡max⁡s∈F(N~(s)−τ~(s))\arg\max_{s\in F}(\tilde N(s)-\tilde\tau(s)): the frontier point farthest above the line joining its cheapest and its richest ends. It is the point of greatest return relative to the frontier's overall trade-off. It is computed but, since it depends on the axis scaling, it is not used for the pick.

10. More objectives: cold, risk, nights

Weather, geography and topography enter the problem through N∗N^* (cloud through T\mathcal T, towns through β\beta, elevation through LM) and through the constraint set (land, roads, reach). Two further concerns came up in the field and are not reducible to NN:

  • Cold. Let κ(s)=−min⁡t∈[t∗,t∗+W]Tapp(s,t)\kappa(s)=-\min_{t\in[t^*,t^*+W]}T_{\mathrm{app}}(s,t), the negative of the coldest forecast apparent temperature during the watch. A cold observer stops looking. This is displayed but not yet optimised.
  • Forecast risk. A forecast of 30% cloud can mean "patchy all night" or "a 30% chance it's overcast". Let σN(s)\sigma_N(s) be the spread of N∗N^* across forecast ensemble members or models. Two sites with equal N∗N^* differ in risk.

The general problem is max⁡s(N∗(s),−τ(s),−κ(s),−σN(s))\max_s\bigl(N^*(s),-\tau(s),-\kappa(s),-\sigma_N(s)\bigr). Definitions 1–2 and Propositions 1, 3 and 4 carry over unchanged to any number of objectives; Proposition 2 (the staircase) is specific to two. The frontier in more dimensions is typically much larger, so the practical route is the ε-constraint method: fix tolerances (for example coldest feels-like ≥ −5 °C, σN/N∗≤0.3\sigma_N/N^*\le 0.3), then solve the bi-objective problem on the sites that pass.

Nights. The app evaluates 7 nights independently. Choosing among nights is the same problem over S×{d1,…,d7}S\times\{d_1,\dots,d_7\}; the seven-night list shows each night's pick, and the best night is the one with the largest UvU_v.

The frontier is exact, so a site can be efficient by a margin that means nothing in practice. The remedy is ε-dominance [9]: treat drive times within, say, 5 minutes as equal, which here would remove the 19.3 site. The three "no" rows lie below the hull edge from home to the 83-minute site, so no single value of vv selects them (Proposition 4).

12. Calibration to one observer

Real observers see fewer meteors than the IMO standard observer, by a roughly constant factor. 14. Limits and threats to validity

  • Light pollution. Walker's law ignores direction of light, shielding by terrain, lighting technology (LED versus sodium) and single large sources (ports, refineries, greenhouses). It was fitted in one region.
  • Horizon. Terrain and trees blocking the sky are not modelled.
  • Access. The model does not know whether a gate is locked, a lot is lit, parking at night is legal, or a road is icy. In the field this mattered more than the model's second decimal.
  • Showers. Fixed parameters per shower; no year-to-year variation, no predicted outbursts from individual dust trails.
  • Cloud. Deterministic fraction (14); layers assumed to overlap at random; no forecast uncertainty.
  • Discretisation. 158 candidates, so the true optimum may lie between them. Refining around the pick would tighten this.
  • Observer. One factor kk per person; no fatigue, no field-of-view or age effects.

15. A protocol to test it

  1. Darkness. Carry an SQM meter to 15–20 sites across a range of predicted SQM. Refit cc and test the residuals. Compare against the VIIRS-based World Atlas [10] at the same points.
  2. Rates. Use IMO standard counting: 15-minute periods, limiting magnitude by star triangle counts, percent of field obscured. Record XiX_i and the model's NiN_i. Test Xi∼Poisson(kNi)X_i\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(kN_i) by Poisson deviance and a dispersion test. Repeat across showers with different rr and speeds.
  3. Historical. Run the model on past nights in the IMO Visual Meteor Database and compare predicted with reported rates, after removing the observer factor.
  4. Decisions. For nights with real counts at more than one site, check whether the frontier ordering of sites matches the observed ordering (rank correlation).
